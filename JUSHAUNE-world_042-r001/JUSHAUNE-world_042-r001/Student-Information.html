Structure & Pacing Plan
A classroom idea bank for building a themed HOME area connected to a playable 2D challenge area.
Students may use a traditional platformer or switch to a similar 2D genre such as a shooter, runner, beat-em-up, top-down action game, puzzle-platformer, or maze game. The important requirement is that the action area makes sense within the story and connects back to the home area.
Choose an idea as a starting point. Change the characters, setting, art style, mechanics, or story to make it your own.

The Structure Plan
A strong small game does not need a huge story. It needs a clear connection between the HOME area, the problem, the playable challenge, and the reward.
1. What is HOME?
The player’s safe area and story hub. Examples: house, village, spaceship, restaurant, garage, castle, school, laboratory, arcade.
2. Who is the PLAYER?
Give the player a simple identity. Example: “I am a robot mechanic who lives in a junkyard.”
3. What does the player CARE ABOUT?
This creates the emotional reason to act: a restaurant, village, pet, friend, family member, spaceship, garden, team, or community.
4. What is WRONG?
This is the antagonism. It can be a villain, rival, corporation, monster, invasion, storm, pollution, broken machine, curse, accident, or time pressure.
5. Why can’t the player just STAY HOME?
Something must force the player into the game area. Example: “My robot needs three power cells, so I have to enter the abandoned factory.”
6. What changes when the player RETURNS HOME?
Show progress. Rescued characters appear, decorations are added, buildings are repaired, dialogue changes, trophies appear, equipment improves, or a new story event unlocks.
Recommended Game Flow
Pacing Diagram
A level should not stay at maximum intensity from beginning to end. Good pacing rises, gives the player a brief rest, then builds toward a climax.
CALM  ->  CHALLENGE  ->  BIGGER CHALLENGE  ->  BREATHER  ->  HARD CHALLENGE  ->  CLIMAX  ->  REWARD
